Evaluation · 6 min read

How evaluators actually mark your answer

What a scoring panel is looking for in the first thirty seconds, and why structure beats prose.

The panel is scoring a document, not your business

A tender evaluator has not met you, does not know your reputation, and is not allowed to give you credit for anything that is not written down. Every mark comes from what is on the page in front of them, scored against a criterion they were given before they read a single submission. Reputation, an existing relationship with the buyer, or a strong sales conversation earlier in the process count for nothing at this stage. If it is not in the answer, it does not exist.

The first thirty seconds decide the tone

Evaluators mark dozens of submissions against the same criteria, often within a tight window. The first few lines of an answer tell them whether they are dealing with a submission that understands the requirement or one that is padding out a word count. Open by answering the question directly, then use the rest of the space to evidence the answer. An opening paragraph that restates the question back at the buyer, or spends three sentences on your company history, has already cost you momentum you will not get back.

Structure beats prose

Most scoring frameworks reward an answer a moderator can mark quickly and consistently. A response broken into clearly labelled sections that mirror the sub-criteria is easier to score highly than a well-written essay that buries the same information in flowing paragraphs. If the question has three parts, answer it in three visible parts. Headings, short paragraphs and the occasional table do more for your score than elegant sentence construction.

Evidence, not adjectives

“Excellent,” “robust” and “proven” are not evidence — they are the words evaluators are trained to discount. What moves a score is a specific example: a contract you delivered, a metric you improved, a method you used and the result it produced. Where you cannot cite a live example, describe the method in enough operational detail that the evaluator can see you have actually done the thinking, not just asserted the outcome.

Answer the question that was asked

It sounds obvious, and it is the single most common reason capable suppliers lose marks. Evaluators mark against the specific wording of the question and the criteria published alongside it, not against what you would have preferred to be asked. If a question asks how you will manage a risk and your answer describes why the risk will not occur, you have answered a different question, and the moderator has nothing to score against the criterion.

Before you submit, check five things

Does every sub-question have a clearly labelled answer. Does every claim have a specific example attached to it. Have you used the buyer’s own terminology rather than your internal jargon. Does the word count serve the answer rather than padding it. And would a stranger who has never spoken to you understand exactly what you are proposing to do, and why it would work, from the page alone.

Social Value · 5 min read

Social value commitments you can evidence

Why unmeasurable pledges lose marks, and how to write commitments you can report against.

Why most social value sections underperform

Social value is usually weighted between ten and twenty per cent of the overall score, and it is also where the most generic writing in a bid tends to end up. A paragraph promising to “support the local community” or “champion diversity and inclusion” reads as a template answer because it almost always is one. Evaluators trained on the social value model are looking for commitments specific enough to be tracked, not sentiment specific enough to sound good.

Aspirational language versus measurable language

There is a simple test for whether a commitment belongs in a bid: could someone check, twelve months from now, whether you did it. “We will create employment opportunities for young people” fails the test. “We will provide two six-month paid work placements for care leavers in the contract area, reported quarterly to the contract manager” passes it. The second version is a longer sentence and a stronger answer, because every word in it is checkable.

Tie every commitment to how it will be reported

A commitment without a reporting mechanism is a promise the buyer has no way of holding you to, and experienced evaluators mark it accordingly. State what you will measure, how often, and who receives the figure. Where the framework references the national Themes, Outcomes and Measures model, use its language and its measures directly rather than translating your own marketing copy into something that sounds similar.

Match the commitment to the contract, not your brochure

A generic corporate social responsibility page copied into a bid is easy to spot and does little for your score, because it was not written for this contract, this authority, or this community. The strongest social value answers respond to something specific in the tender documents — the local area, the sector’s known skills gap, the authority’s published strategy — and commit to something proportionate to the size of the contract you are actually bidding for.

A short example

Weak: “We are committed to reducing our environmental impact throughout the contract.” Strong: “We will reduce contract-related fleet mileage by switching two delivery routes to an electric vehicle within the first six months, reporting quarterly mileage and emissions figures to the contract manager.” Same intention — only one of them can be checked.

What to check before it goes in

Every commitment should survive one question: if the authority rang the person responsible for it in a year’s time, could they report a number. If the honest answer is no, rewrite the commitment until it can, or leave it out — a shorter, evidenced social value section consistently scores better than a longer aspirational one.

Getting Started · 7 min read

Your first public-sector bid: a checklist

The documents, policies and insurances to have ready before you open a portal.

Before you look for an opportunity

Most of the work that decides whether you can bid at all happens before a single tender notice appears. Contracting authorities disqualify capable suppliers every week for a missing mandatory document, not for a weak technical answer. Getting the foundations in place first means you can move on a live opportunity in days rather than weeks.

The documents to have ready

At minimum: current insurance certificates (public and employer’s liability, and professional indemnity if your sector requires it), a health and safety policy appropriate to your size, any sector accreditations you hold (CHAS, SafeContractor, ISO certifications), your most recently filed accounts, method statements and risk assessments for your core service, two or three case studies of similar work with a named reference, CVs for the staff who would deliver the contract, and evidence of right to work and any required vetting arrangements. Buyers ask for the same handful of documents in almost every tender; keep them current in one folder rather than assembling them from scratch each time.

Register on the portals that matter

Public contracts are published across several platforms rather than one central list: the Central Digital Platform (Find a Tender for higher-value contracts), Contracts Finder, and a number of regional and sector-specific portals that individual authorities and frameworks still use directly. Register your company profile on each early — most require basic company information and a short verification step, and you do not want to be doing this for the first time against a live deadline.

Read the invitation to tender properly before you commit

The pricing structure, the evaluation criteria, the weightings and the mandatory pass/fail questions are usually set out clearly in the first few pages of the tender documents, and they tell you almost everything about whether the contract is winnable for a business your size. Read these before you commit time to a full response. A contract weighted heavily on price, or one that requires an accreditation you do not hold, is worth knowing about on day one rather than after a week of writing.

Make a genuine go or no-go decision

Not every published opportunity is worth bidding for. Weigh the size of the contract against your delivery capacity, whether you meet every mandatory requirement, how many competitors are likely to be well placed for it, and what it would cost you in staff time to write a competitive response. A deliberate decision not to bid, made early, is a better use of your time than a rushed submission made against a deadline you were never going to meet well.

What happens after you submit

Most authorities publish a response date and a debrief process, win or lose. If you are unsuccessful, request the scored feedback — it is usually available on request and is the most specific information you will ever get about how your submission compared to the winning one. Treat every submission, successful or not, as evidence you can reuse and improve for the next one.

Policies

What We Hold, and What We Do Not

Public summaries of the policies we operate. Each describes what we actually do at our size. We deliberately do not claim structures we do not operate — a policy describing arrangements that do not exist is a scoring risk and, in a declaration, a misrepresentation. Full documents are available to clients and to contracting authorities on request.

M1

Equality, Diversity and Inclusion

We do not discriminate on any of the nine characteristics protected by the Equality Act 2010. Every role is advertised against a written specification before any candidate is considered, and criteria are limited to what the role genuinely requires — not the institution attended, the year of graduation, or an unbroken employment history. Reasonable adjustments are offered at the point of application and a request for one is never a factor in the decision.

In the work itself: documents use structural headings, meaningful table headers, text alternatives and sufficient contrast, and never rely on colour alone to convey meaning. Where we draft an equality response for a client we record only arrangements the client actually operates.

M2

Environmental and Sustainability

We are a remote-first micro-entity: no manufacturing plant, no vehicle fleet, no waste carrier licence, no dedicated commercial premises. Our material impacts are home-workplace electricity, business travel, IT hardware lifecycle, cloud storage and paper. Remote meetings are the default, rail is preferred over car and domestic air, hardware runs a minimum four-year replacement cycle, and submissions and contracts are electronic unless an authority requires hard copy.

The policy is aligned to the principles of ISO 14001:2015. We are not certified to that standard and do not represent ourselves as certified. We structure and draft Carbon Reduction Plans; we do not calculate greenhouse gas emissions.

M3

Quality Management

Quality means four things. Compliant — the submission meets every mandatory requirement, in the format required, before the deadline; a brilliant response rejected on a compliance failure has no value. Accurate — every factual statement is true and evidenced. Deliverable — a commitment we draft is one you have confirmed you can perform; winning on an undeliverable promise is a loss deferred. Useful — you are left better able to bid next time.

Structured to follow ISO 9001:2015 so that an authority can map it clause by clause. We are not certified to that standard. We give no guarantee that any tender will be successful.

M4

Safeguarding

We do not provide care, education or support services, and nothing we do is regulated activity within the meaning of the Safeguarding Vulnerable Groups Act 2006. We hold this policy because our clients include care, supported housing, education and NHS-adjacent providers, we attend their premises, and the documentation they send us — incident logs, CQC and Ofsted reports, complaints and staffing records — routinely contains safeguarding information.

Encountering a concern in a document triggers a duty to act just as encountering it in a room does. Our responsibility is to recognise, record and report. It is not to investigate, assess or intervene, and the policy is written so nobody acting for us mistakes the one for the other.
